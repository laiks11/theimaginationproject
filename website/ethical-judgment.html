The skill of holding two honest answers at once.

This month's challenge is a short dilemma with no easy answer, built to be argued from more than one decent point of view. Below it sits everything you need to think it through on your own: the lenses, the words, a four-week path, and where to read further.

Skill
Ethical judgment
Reading level
Grades 5–9
Time
45–60 min
Materials
Paper & pen
This month's dilemma

The Wrong Name

It happens on a Tuesday, the morning after the history exam. Your best friend leans over at lunch and tells you something quietly, almost relieved to finally say it out loud. They copied half their answers from a sheet they had hidden, and they got an A. You promise not to tell, and you mean it. They are your closest friend, and they trusted you with the truth.

Then, in third period, the teacher stops the class. She is certain someone cheated, because the answers were too alike to be chance. And she names a student — but the wrong one. A quiet kid named Daniel, who studied for a week and copied nothing. He is about to lose his grade, and maybe his place on the team, for something he did not do.

You know exactly what happened. You also gave your word. Say nothing, and an innocent person is punished. Speak up, and you betray the friend who trusted you. There is no move here that costs nothing — and that is the point.

Your task

Decide what you would honestly do. Then build the strongest, fairest argument you can for the choice you did not make. A real thinker can defend the side they walked away from.

Two honest sides

Neither side is the easy one.

Before you pick, read both cases as if you believed them. A dilemma is only worth the name when each path asks you to give up something real.

Keep your friend's trust

A promise is a promise, and loyalty is not nothing.

Your friend confided in you. If a promise only holds when it is convenient, it was never worth much. Betraying them does real, lasting harm to someone you love — and it may not even be your secret to tell. There are other ways to help Daniel without naming your friend.

What kind of friend breaks a confidence the moment it gets hard?

Protect the innocent

An innocent person is about to pay for someone else's choice.

Daniel did nothing wrong, and your silence is what convicts him. A promise made to hide the truth cannot outweigh real harm to a person who trusted the system to be fair. Loyalty to one friend does not cancel your duty to everyone else in the room.

What kind of person lets a stranger take the fall to keep themselves comfortable?

The toolkit

Three lenses for any hard choice.

Philosophers have argued about right and wrong for thousands of years, and most of it comes down to three questions. Try each one on the dilemma and notice how the answer changes.

Lens one

Consequences

What actually happens to people?

Follow each choice to its end. Who is helped, who is hurt, and how much? On this view, the right action is the one that leads to the best overall outcome, even if it is uncomfortable.

Consequentialism
Lens two

Duties

What is right, no matter the result?

Some things hold regardless of outcome: keep your word, tell the truth, do not let the innocent suffer. When two duties collide — as they do here — you have to decide which one runs deeper.

Deontology
Lens three

Character

Who do I become by choosing this?

Every choice is also practice. Ask not only what to do, but what kind of person each option makes you — loyal, brave, honest, cowardly — and who you want to be when it is over.

Virtue ethics
How to argue it well

The goal is not to win. It is to be fair.

Good ethical judgment is not about being the loudest. It is about seeing a problem clearly, treating every side honestly, and being able to say why.

  1. Name the stakeholders

    List everyone the choice touches — your friend, Daniel, the teacher, the class, you. A choice that only considers one person is not finished.

  2. Try all three lenses

    Run the dilemma through consequences, duties, and character. Write one sentence for each. Notice where they agree and where they pull apart.

  3. Steelman the other side

    Build the strongest version of the argument you disagree with — not a weak one that is easy to knock down. If you cannot, you do not understand it yet.

  4. Take a stand, and own the cost

    Choose. Then say plainly what your choice gives up. An honest position names its own price instead of pretending there is none.

Sentence starters
  • The people most affected by this are…
  • If I only look at the consequences, then…
  • The duty that matters most here is… because…
  • The strongest argument against me is…
  • I would choose …, and the price of that choice is…
Words for your toolkit

The word bank.

Sixteen terms to research and reach for. Use them in your argument, and keep this sheet beside you as you read further.

Ethics
Thinking carefully about what is right and wrong, and being able to say why.
Moral dilemma
A situation where every choice means giving up something that genuinely matters.
Consequentialism
Judging an action by its results: the right choice is the one that leads to the best outcome.
Deontology dee-on-TOL-uh-jee
Judging an action by the duty behind it: some things are right or wrong whatever the result.
Virtue ethics
Asking not only “what should I do?” but “what kind of person does this make me?”
Principle
A rule you hold to even when it is hard, like “tell the truth” or “keep your promises.”
Intuition
The gut sense that something is right or wrong, before you can explain it in words.
Stakeholder
Anyone affected by a choice. Here: your friend, Daniel, the teacher, the class, you.
The golden rule
Treat others the way you would want to be treated if you stood in their place.
Veil of ignorance
A test: choose as if you did not yet know which person in the story you would turn out to be.
Moral courage
Doing the right thing precisely when it costs you something to do it.
Integrity
Living by your values even when no one is watching and no one would know.
Justice
Giving each person what they are genuinely owed; fairness, applied without favorites.
Loyalty
Standing by the people who have placed their trust in you.
Legal vs. ethical
The law says what you must do; ethics asks what you should do. They are not always the same.
Steelman
Building the strongest possible version of an argument you disagree with before you answer it.
A four-week path

Build the skill, one week at a time.

For students working alone, families at the table, or a teacher with one short session a week. No preparation, no screens required.

Week oneNotice

Tell a dilemma from an easy choice.

Most choices are not dilemmas — one option is simply better. Collect three situations from your own week and decide which are genuine dilemmas, where every path costs something.

DoRead the challenge and write, in one line, what each choice gives up.
Week twoLenses

Look through all three.

Take the dilemma and run it through consequences, duties, and character in turn. The point is not to agree with all three, but to see how each one reframes the same facts.

DoWrite one honest sentence per lens about The Wrong Name.
Week threeSteelman

Argue the side you disagree with.

Pick the choice you like least and make the best possible case for it. Borrow nobody's strawman. This is the week that separates judgment from opinion.

DoWrite a short paragraph defending the side you would not choose.
Week fourDecide

Take a stand and meet the objection.

Now choose, for real, and say why. Then answer the single strongest objection to your view honestly — without pretending your choice was free of cost.

DoWrite your position, name its price, and respond to one objection.
